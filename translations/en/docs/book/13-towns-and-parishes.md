# A New Life: Towns and Parishes

Not everyone went looking for new ploughland. From the second half of the 19th century, bearers of the surname appear in towns, in mines and factories, in military service and among parish clergy, from Kyiv to Orenburg. On paper most of them long remained peasants of their home villages, even when they lived and worked hundreds of versts away.

{{STORY_MAP:new-life}}

## Crimea after the Crimean War

After the Crimean War, in 1860–1862, tens of thousands of Crimean Tatars left the peninsula, and the deserted lands began to be settled with peasants from the central provinces. In 1868 in Simferopol, the chief town of Taurida Province, eight-day-old **[Matrona](/records/FS-3Q9M-CS9N-9FBF)** died. She was the daughter of **Sergey Ampilogov**, a state peasant of **Rylsk District, Kursk Province**.

## A Soldier's Service in Orenburg

**Gordey Grigoryevich Anpilogov** was born in 1849 near Orenburg and in 1867 [was married in Karmalka](/records/YA-90DBF5AD-69). By the mid-1870s he was already in Orenburg, in military service: in 1876 a [non-commissioned officer of the local battalion](/records/YA-OGAOO-173-16-216-9-GORDEY), in 1877 a [sergeant major of the military hospital](/records/YA-OGAOO-173-12-831-69-GORDEY), then a warden of the commissariat depot, a [gendarme non-commissioned officer](/records/YA-OGAOO-173-16-496-136-PARASKEVA), and again a sergeant major at army depots. The family raised its children in the town, but on paper Gordey was still registered as a [peasant of Karmalka](/records/YA-OGAOO-173-16-612-139-GORDEY).

## Outsiders on the Don

The Don Host Province lived by its own rules: the land there belonged to the Cossack Host and the stanitsas (Cossack settlements). Peasants who came from other provinces were called **inogorodnie** ("outsiders", literally "people from other towns"). They were not entitled to a Cossack land share, so they rented land or hired themselves out for work: in the fields, in the mines, in the towns.

**Yegor Vladimirovich Anpilogov** was one such outsider: not a Cossack but a peasant of Fatezh District, Kursk Province. In 1891, in Taganrog District, he and his wife Vera Alekseyevna [had a son, Grigory](/records/FS-3QS7-89J5-T4BJ).

## Mines and Factories of the Donbas

Next door, in Bakhmut District of Yekaterinoslav Province, lay the heart of the Donbas. In the last third of the 19th century coal mines and ironworks opened there one after another, and there were never enough hands. In 1900 Yekaterina was born in Druzhkovka, the daughter of **[Pavel Ivanovich Anpilogov from Radovka, Rylsk District](/records/FS-3QS7-L9JR-BDGY)**. A Bakhmut record of 1910 names **[Gavriil Yefimovich Anpilogov from Pokrovka, Korocha District](/records/FS-3QS7-89JT-6H5R)**. Like Yegor Vladimirovich, both had come from Kursk Province.

## Peasants in the Town

The towns grew fast: people went there for wages, schooling and service. Yet registration with the village community was not changed for years, so a townsman in his way of life often stayed a peasant on paper. In 1901 in Samara **Grigory Mikhaylovich Anpilogov**, a peasant of Bugulma District, [was married](/records/FS-9392-MLW4-6).

Sometimes the move into the townspeople can be seen right in the documents. **Aleksey Avraamovich Anpilogov** of Kromy District, Oryol Province, in 1907 [has his daughter Lyubov baptised](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSSV-K9K7-G) in Novogrigoryevka, Yekaterinoslav Province, still as a peasant. But in 1915, when [his daughter Valentina is baptised](/records/FS-3Q9M-CS9B-99JB-6), he is already a Melitopol meshchanin, that is, a registered townsman of Melitopol. Valentina's godfather was a man from the same parts, also a native of Kromy District.

**Yegor Kosmich Anpilogov** was from the same district: in Kyiv in 1916, at the height of the First World War, [his son **Boris** was born](/records/FS-3Q9M-CS9T-TS88-Q), while the father was still registered as a peasant of Kromy District.

## Among the Village Clergy

There were bearers of the surname among the rural clergy as well. In 1913, in the village of Slonovka near Orenburg, the parish register entries were certified by **Deacon Afinogen Ampilogov**. In 1915 in Karmalka, where the descendants of the Kursk settlers lived, the entries were signed by **Dmitry Ampilogov**, a deacon serving in the post of psalm-reader, who kept the parish register. Every birth, marriage and death in the village passed through their hands.

## A Family of Servitors

The ploughmen, workers and deacons of the 19th century descended from people of a quite different standing. The early documents find the surname above all among men in state service. From the end of the 16th century Anfilogovs, Onpilogovs and Anpilogovs were **deti boyarskie** ("boyars' sons"), the lower stratum of the service gentry. They served the Muscovite sovereigns from Fyodor Ioannovich to Peter I: they turned out for musters, marched with the regiments against Crimean raids, and moved to the new fortresses (Oryol, Kursk, Oskol, Oboyan) wherever the frontier ran. For their service they received service estates (pomestya), but there were hardly any peasants on them: in the Oryol cadastral survey book (pistsovaya kniga) of 1594/95, nine landholders have not a single inhabited peasant household between them, and they ploughed the land themselves.

They did not serve only as deti boyarskie. In 1637 Onpilogovs joined the regimental mounted Cossacks of the new fortress of Userd; in 1647 a regimental Cossack Danila Anpilov is mentioned at Oskol. In the second half of the 17th century the Oryol and Kursk Anpilogovs served as reiters and pikemen in the new-order regiments, in the 18th century in the landmilitia of the Ukrainian Line, and in the 19th century many of them near Orenburg became Cossacks of the Orenburg Host. The documents so far know no Zaporozhian or Don Cossacks among them.

Peter I replaced the service-estate cavalry with a regular army, and in the poll-tax census of 1719–1724 most descendants of the servitors were registered as **odnodvortsy** (single-household servitors). They kept their ancestors' land, but they paid the poll tax, served in the landmilitia, and in status drew ever closer to the state peasants, with whom they finally merged in 1866. Only one branch, the descendants of the Oryol syn boyarsky Vasily Dmitriyevich, managed to prove ancient nobility and was entered in the genealogical book of the Kursk Province nobility. By the 19th century the rest were registered as state peasants and meshchane (townspeople), and in that status they left for new lands, for the towns and for the clergy.

## Today

Ampilogovs and Anpilogovs now live all over Russia, from the western regions to the Far East, in the south and in the Far North, as well as in other countries. Many branches are not yet linked to one another by documents. How to find your own branch and check the records that lead to it is the subject of [the last chapter](/read/book/14-method).
