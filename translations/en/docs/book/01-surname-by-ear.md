# A Surname Written Down by Ear

Ampilogov, Anpilogov, Anfilogov, Onfilogov. They look like four different surnames, but they share a single origin: the Greek name **Amphilochos**. It was the name of a seer from ancient legend, and later of a fourth-century bishop whom the Church entered into the svyattsy — the calendar of saints from which names were given in Rus — as **Amphilochius**.

In Rus the bookish name was reshaped to suit everyday speech. Old Russian had no *f* sound; it was replaced with *p*, and so the name became **Ampilog**, and alongside it **Anpilog**. The son of Anpilog was called Anpilogov ("Anpilog's"). Once the grandsons came to be called the same way, the patronymic turned into a surname. This explanation was given by the linguist V. A. Nikonov.

## Onfilogov in July, Anfilogov in October

There was no fixed spelling of surnames at the time. A man gave his name, the clerk wrote it down as he heard it — and the next time he might hear it differently.

In the summer of 1626 the servitors of Oryol — men who served the tsar with horse and weapons in return for land or pay — were stationed with the regiments at Mtsensk, some fifty kilometres from Oryol, guarding the southern districts against Crimean raids. They were inspected twice: in July, when they began their tour of duty, and in October, before they were dismissed. [In the July list](/records/RGADA-210-12-13-L105) Ivan Vasilyevich and Mark Karpovich are Onfilogovs; [in the October one](/records/RGADA-210-12-13-L149) they are already Anfilogovs. The same men, the same service, three months apart. Yet Aleksey Vasilyevich, who stood next to Ivan in both lists, remained an Onfilogov.

Two and a half centuries later nothing had changed. In 1891 Grigory Andreyevich, a peasant from Timoshevka near Melitopol, [is married as Ampilogov](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSS9-N7LM-D). A year later, in the register entry for the birth of his daughter Pelageya, the father is [recorded as Anpilov — and someone has written «го» ("-go") above the word](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSSW-89J7-W), turning it into Anpilogov. Agafiya is baptised in 1896 [as the daughter of Anpilov](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSSW-QSY7-L), and her sister Yelena in 1897 [once again as the daughter of Ampilogov](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSSX-4CTH-F). The same parents, six years — and three surnames.

Hence the variety of forms: **Ampilogov, Anpilogov, Anfilogov, Onfilogov, Onpilogov, Ampelogov, Anpelogov**. The first letter wavered between *A* and *O*, *f* gave way to *p*, *i* to *e*. A different spelling does not by itself make people strangers to one another.

## From the Volga to the Crimea

The first known record of the family form was made in 1536/37. In the village of Priluki on the Volga, owned by the Trinity-Sergius Monastery, a census-taker named the inhabitant of one of the households: [«Тонкой Онфилогов» (Tonkoy Onfilogov)](/records/PUB-ZOR40-1536-PRILUKI-P43).

By the end of the sixteenth century the surname is already on the southern frontier of the Muscovite state. The country was then pushing out into the steppe: it built fortresses against Crimean raids and gathered servitors there from every corner. In the Oryol district [nine servitors](/read/book/05-orel-1594-1596) hold land in return for service. In 1651 [Sergey Anpilogov](/read/book/07-kursk-oboyan-1636-1651) receives five roubles and undertakes to live by the new fortress of Oboyan: to build a house, plough the land, and keep a horse and weapons. In 1700 [Akim Anpilogov](/read/book/08-akim-bazdyrevo-shchigry-1700), in a written statement, recalls a lost estate near Oryol and his grandson's land in the Livny district. Later one of the branches gathers papers to [confirm its noble descent](/read/book/09-anfilogov-nobility).

The surname also remains on the map — in the names of the villages [Anpilogovo](/read/book/10-anpilogovo-kursk) and [Anpilovka](/read/book/11-anpilovka-stary-oskol). From the eighteenth century its bearers move to [new lands](/read/book/12-new-lands) — to the Ukrainian Line and New Russia, to the Caucasus, the Orenburg region, the lower Volga and Siberia, and later to the [towns](/read/book/13-towns-and-parishes). People left for these places to escape the crowding of the central provinces, and there they are found in parish records of births, marriages and deaths.

## One Name Is Not Yet One Family

No common ancestor of all these people has been found so far. Perhaps there never was one: the same name could have given rise to a surname in different families in different places. That is why kinship appears in this book only where a document confirms it.

If you bear one of these surnames, your branch may turn up in any chapter — or may still be waiting for its document. How to carry out the search yourself is explained in the [last chapter](/read/book/14-method).

But let us begin with the name itself — and with a boy on a Greek vase, painted more than two and a half thousand years ago.
