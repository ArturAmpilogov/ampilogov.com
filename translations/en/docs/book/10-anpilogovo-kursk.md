# Anpilogovo: One Name, Different Villages

Near Kursk there is an Anpilogovo even today — two of them, in fact: **1st and 2nd Anpilogovo** in Kursk District. Both are living villages: according to the 2010 census, 180 people lived in the first and 142 in the second.

{{ANPILOGOVO_MAP}}

The villages received their numbers only in Soviet times, at the turn of the 1920s and 1930s. In the 19th century two villages called Anpilogova stood here, and quite different people lived in them.

**State Anpilogova** — small, with 16 households in 1862 — was a village of the Anpilogov odnodvortsy, free state peasants.

**Proprietary Anpilogova** — large, with 59 households and 707 inhabitants — belonged to landowners, and until 1861 its inhabitants were serfs. It was settled by families with other surnames: some of them the landowners had bought in 1781 on another estate and resettled here.

## The Name Outlived Its Owners

In 1642 an allotment book (otkaznaya kniga) — a document recording the transfer of land — states that [Vasily Anpilogov had formerly lived in the village of Anpilogova](/records/PUB-KOTKOV-1977-P171-P172-VASILY-GAVRILA-ANPILOGOV), on the service estate of his uncle Gavriil. The village, it turns out, bore the name of its holders. But by 1642 Gavriil's land had already been handed out to three other Kursk deti boyarskie (petty military servitors). A service estate (pomestye) was not private property, after all: if its holder died without heirs or stopped serving, his land was given to others. Some twenty-odd years later Ilya Kharlanov is recorded as the landholder here, yet the village is still called Onpilogova.

## The Odnodvortsy

From 1719 to 1858 the state periodically counted everyone who paid taxes. These censuses were called revisions, and the name-by-name lists of inhabitants were called revision tales (revizskie skazki). In the revision tale of 1816, State Anpilogova has **16 households**, all of them Anpilogovs, and all of them odnodvortsy.

That was the name given to the descendants of the servicemen of the southern frontier. The word comes from «одного двора» ("of one household"): a minor serviceman often had no peasants, and his whole holding fitted into a single household of his own. The frontier had long since moved south, and Peter I had replaced the old service-estate cavalry with a regular army. During the poll-tax census of 1719–1724, the minor servicemen of the southern districts who had not made it into the nobility were grouped together as odnodvortsy: they began to pay the poll tax and, from 1723, a further 40 kopecks per soul for the upkeep of the landmilitia, a settled militia force in which they themselves served. The land their ancestors had received for service stayed with the families, and until 1840 odnodvortsy could even own serfs, although few of them had any.

The odnodvortsy lived like peasants — they ploughed their own fields and paid taxes — but they held their land in hereditary shares, «четвертями» ("by quarters"), and divided it among their sons. In 1866 they were finally reclassified as state peasants, but the quarter-holding households remained: in 1872 there were 15 such households there, and 11 of them belonged to Anpilogovs. More than two hundred years after the allotment book, the family name was still living on its own land.

{{RESEARCH_NOTES}}

**1642 and 1664.** Allotment book of 28 January 1642: [the record of Vasily and Gavriil](/records/PUB-KOTKOV-1977-P171-P172-VASILY-GAVRILA-ANPILOGOV). In the published heading of the 1664 census the village is called Onpilogova and the landholder is Ilya Kharlanov; the heading does not list all the inhabitants on the two leaves and does not prove that the Anpilogovs had disappeared from there. [Census card](/records/PUB-RGADA-1209-1-9768-KURSK-ONPILOGOVA-1664). [S-ANPILOGOVO-1642]

**The odnodvortsy.** As a distinct category they took shape from various groups of the southern service population at the beginning of the 18th century; the origin of any particular branch is confirmed by that branch's own documents. The poll tax, the landmilitia levy from 1723, the right to own serfs until 1840 and the reclassification as state peasants in 1866 follow a general reference on the estate ([Wikipedia](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Однодворцы)). Revision tale of 1816: [S-GAKO-184-2-380-ANPILOGOVA-1816]. [S-ODNODVORTSY]

**Two villages.** The 1862 gazetteer: State Anpilogova in Fatezh District — 16 households, 56 inhabitants; Proprietary Anpilogova in Kursk District — 59 households, 707 inhabitants. [S-SNMR-1862-TWO-ANPILOGOVA]. Working geolocations place the villages about one and a half kilometres apart. The settling of serf families is visible from a deed of purchase of 1781: according to A. V. Zorin's analysis, the landowner Pereverzev placed some of the purchased families in Anpilogovo, Bukreevo and Kasinovo. [S-GAKO-F59-O1-D749-ANPILOGOVO-1781]

**Quarter-right tenure.** The 1872 count was published by N. A. Blagoveshchensky in his book «Четвертное право» (Chetvertnoe pravo, "Quarter Right", 1899): 15 quarter-holding households — 11 Anpilogovs, 2 Kalugins, 1 Makovnev and 1 Durneva. The author's conclusion about a common original kin group is a reconstruction from land history, not a name-by-name genealogy. [S-BLAGOVESHCHENSKY-1872-ANPILOGOVA]

**1st and 2nd Anpilogovo.** In 1918 and 1926 the records name a single Anpilogovo (192 farmsteads and 1,094 inhabitants in 1918), while the 1934 gazetteer already lists two numbered villages. Both names are still official; the population figures are from the 2010 census. How they relate to the two communities of the 19th century remains to be established from land-survey plans and revisions; it is too early to explain them as the simple division of one village. [S-ANPILOGOVO-DIVISION]

**Other Kursk centres.** In Sakolova the revision of 1762 names the households of Ivan Dementyevich and Gavriil Tarasovich Anpilogov and of Ivan Larionovich Onpilogov; their connection with Anpilogova has not been established. [S-GAKO-1762-SAKOLOVA-NIKOLSKOE]
