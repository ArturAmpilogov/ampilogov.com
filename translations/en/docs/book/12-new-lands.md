# New Lands: Where the Families Went

For two centuries in a row, bearers of the surname pulled up stakes. Some were sent to the frontier by the state itself; others left the crowding behind for places where land was plentiful: the Ukrainian Line, New Russia (Novorossiya), the Caucasus, the Orenburg region, the lower Volga and Siberia.

{{STORY_MAP:migration-overview}}

## Why They Left

By the end of the 18th century the Kursk and Oryol governorates had grown crowded. Families grew, but the land around the villages did not: each new generation divided the same fields among an ever larger number of sons and households, and the strips grew ever narrower. Officials had a short word for it — **malozemelye** (land shortage).

On the borderlands everything was the other way round: there lay vast free lands that the state wanted to settle and hold. The treasury moved state peasants (kazyonnye krestyane, that is, peasants belonging to the state) there from the crowded governorates and granted the settlers privileges. A state peasant could not simply leave of his own accord: he was registered in his rural community and paid taxes together with it. Resettlement therefore went through petitions, through scouts (khodoki) who first inspected the land, and through permission from the treasury. Serfs the treasury could not resettle in this way: they belonged to landowners.

After the abolition of serfdom in 1861 the crowding only grew. Allotments often turned out smaller than before, there were more people with every generation, and land that had been ploughed for centuries almost without manure was becoming exhausted. So began the second wave of resettlement — again to the south, and then to Siberia as well.

## To the South: the Ukrainian Line and New Russia

{{STORY_MAP:south-roads}}

The first great road led south while the Crimean raids were still going on. In the 1730s Russia built the Ukrainian Line — a rampart with fortresses between the Dnieper and the Seversky Donets, meant to stop Tatar raiding parties. It was guarded by the landmilitia — a force of odnodvortsy (single-household servitors), who served in turns and the rest of the time ploughed their own land. The landmilitia database for 1732–1755 holds [26 entries for Anpilogovs](/records/PUB-UKRAINIAN-LINE-LANDMILITIA-ANPILOGOV-NAMED-1732-1755), most of them from Kromy and Kursk districts. From 1732 some odnodvortsy were resettled right up to the Line: families received land by the fortresses. From Kursk district [five Anpilogovs went there](/records/PUB-UKRAINIAN-LINE-COLONISTS-ANPILOGOV-NAMED-1732-1764); the household of one of them, Fyodor Ivanovich, is recorded in the 1760s–1770s in the sloboda (settlement) by the Ryazhsk fortress.

In 1783 Russia annexed Crimea, and the Crimean Khanate ceased to exist. The raids stopped, and the vast steppes north of the peninsula at last became safe for ploughing. There was plenty of land there and few ploughmen. In 1810 state peasants from five districts of Kursk governorate were settled around a khutor (farmstead) near Melitopol — and so **Tymoshivka** grew up. Here in 1836 **[Ilya Ioannovich Anpilogov](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSS9-SSRL-W)** died aged 85, and the parish registers mention adult men with the patronymic Ilyich — apparently his sons. The family of Grigory Andreyevich from the first chapter also came from Tymoshivka.

A year later 70 male souls of state peasants of Kromy district asked to be moved to free state land in Kherson governorate. The list includes [two large Anpilogov families](/records/RGIA-F1285-O1-D131-CHEREMOSHNAYA-ANPILOGOV-1811) from the village of Cheremoshnaya, the households of Yakov Laktionovich and Stefan Anikeyevich: with wives, children, a daughter-in-law, a grandson and an inventory of property.

The second wave after 1861 again went south. In 1870 **Kuzma and Vasily Anpilogov**, together with other Kursk families, were being enrolled in the village of **Otrada** on the left bank of the lower Dnieper, that is, admitted to the rural community with an allotment and taxes. And in the village of Privolnoye in Kherson governorate in 1887 Ivan was born, son of **[Gavriil Ivanovich Anpilogov](/records/FS-3Q9M-CS9N-J3NL-F)**, a peasant of **Medyn district, Kaluga governorate**: this family came not from the black-earth south but from near Kaluga.

## The Caucasus: to Free State Lands

{{STORY_MAP:caucasus}}

In the 1820s the state was settling the Caucasus Province — the steppes of Ciscaucasia, which it needed to secure for itself. Kursk peasants wrote petitions. In 1826 [Semyon Anpilogov](/records/RGIA-F379-O1-D742-TETEREVINO-SEMEN-ANPILOGOV-1826) from the village of Teterevino in Korocha district asked to be resettled there. In 1827, 213 families of Fatezh district, among them [four Anpilogovs](/records/RGIA-F379-O1-D856-NIKOLSKOE-ANPILOGOV-1827) from the village of Nikolskoye near Knyazhyi, asked, because of land shortage, to be allotted free state lands in the Caucasus Province.

One such road has been traced to the end. In 1826 a case was opened on the resettlement of odnodvortsy of Pavlovsk district, Voronezh governorate, and among them were [the families of Yemelyan Nikitich and Leonty Fedoseyevich Anpilogov](/records/RGIA-F379-O1-D731-ANPILOGOV-TIKHORETSKOE-1826) from the villages of Verkhny Mamon and Buylovo. The final note reports that the settlers «поселились на речке Тихой, селение Тихорецкое» (settled on the Tikhaya stream, the settlement of Tikhoretskoye). The settlement later became a stanitsa (Cossack village) — today it is the stanitsa of Fastovetskaya in Krasnodar Krai — and [the Ampilogovs were still living there in the 1840s](/records/PUB-GASK-TIKHORETSKOE-AMPILOGOV-HOUSEHOLDS-1835-1848).

## The Orenburg Region: Ploughmen and Cossacks

{{STORY_MAP:orenburg}}

There was free land in the east as well — in the Orenburg region by the Southern Urals, a former borderland covered by a chain of fortresses along the Ural River. It was here that the surname found its largest new homeland.

In 1828 a group of settlers from Kursk governorate arrived near Orenburg. It was led by **[Larion Anpilogov](/records/NARB-ZOBOV-SARMANAEVA-ANPILOGOV-1828)** — the poverenny, that is, the elected representative who negotiated with the authorities on behalf of everyone. With him came his 70-year-old father Ivan Sergeyevich and his brothers Nikifor and Boris. A year later odnodvortsy from Kromy district of Oryol governorate settled nearby, among them four **[Anpilogov households](/records/NARB-ZOBOV-SARMANAEVA-ANPILOGOV-1829)**. The settlers were allotted the land of the neighbouring village of Sarmanayeva, and here **Karmalka** grew up — today a village in Sharlyk district of Orenburg Oblast.

After that Kursk families came here one after another. In 1832 six Anpilogovs from Nikolskoye near Knyazhyi were on a [list of state settlers](/records/RGIA-F379-O1-D1206-NIKOLSKOE-ANPILOGOV-1832) who wanted to send scouts to inspect the free lands of Orenburg governorate. From 1837 state peasants were administered by Count P. D. Kiselyov's new Ministry of State Properties, and resettlement from the crowded governorates became its constant concern. In 1838 its [register of land-short peasants](/records/RGIA-F383-O1-D299-BREKHOVA-ANPILOGOV-1838) named three Anpilogovs from the village of Brekhova in Fatezh district, and the reason is written down plainly: the families did not have enough land in their old home. Prokhor Matveyevich from this list soon appears in the parish registers of Karmalka and Orenburg. Kursk settlers also took root in Buzuluk district, then also part of Orenburg governorate: in 1840 in Zaplavnoye there is a record of [Lev Anpilogov «из переселян»](/records/PUB-OGAOO-173-15-443-LEV-ANPILOGOV-MIGRANT-1840) (from the settlers) of the village of Anpilogova in Fatezh district.

The region was guarded by the Orenburg Cossack Host. The Statute of 1840 fixed its lands and the order of service, and the Host was replenished not only with the descendants of Cossacks but also with retired soldiers and peasants of the region. Many bearers of the surname ended up in its ranks: in the 1840s, in the village of Larionovka and in the Kaldyayevsky detachment of Chernootrozhskaya stanitsa, east of Orenburg, whole families of Ampilogovs and Anpilogovs are recorded as Cossacks. The Anpilogovs of the neighbouring Izyak-Nikitinsky settlement were Cossacks too, and in the 1890s Ksenofont Anpilogov served as an uryadnik — a junior commander — in the 1st Department of the Host. Other families, as in Karmalka, remained state peasants.

The settlers' descendants stayed here for a long time. The parish registers of Karmalka, neighbouring Slonovka, the Cossack settlements and Orenburg itself are full of their births, weddings and burials: more than four thousand records from the Orenburg region for the 1820s–1910s have been collected for this book.

## The Lower Volga: Bakhtemir

{{STORY_MAP:volga-delta}}

In 1837 the family of [Mikhail Anpilogov](/records/YA-GAAO-454-1-339-242-MIKHAIL) — with his brother Timofey and a nephew — moved from Yenotayevsk district, further up the Volga, to the village of Bakhtemir in the very delta of the river and was enrolled among the local state peasants. The delta lived on fish: its fisheries were among the richest in the country, and working hands were always needed there. The family took root: in the parish registers of Bakhtemir the surname appears right up to 1917.

## Siberia

{{STORY_MAP:siberia}}

The farthest road was Siberia. In the 1880s Kursk peasants asked more and more often to go beyond the Urals: in 1887 peasants of several volosts (rural districts) of Kursk, Lgov and Fatezh districts submitted a petition for resettlement to Tomsk governorate, and among them were more than thirty inhabitants of the village of Anpilogova. In 1889 the law on voluntary resettlement put in order the permits and privileges for those who went to state lands, and from 1891 the Trans-Siberian Railway was being built, so that a journey that used to take months was becoming shorter. After 1906, under Prime Minister P. A. Stolypin, the treasury gave settlers loans for the journey and marked out land for them: within a few years millions of people moved beyond the Urals.

Anpilogovs and Anpilovs from Fatezh, Grayvoron and Stary Oskol districts [left](/records/PUB-GAKO-68-1-ANPILOGOV-RESETTLERS-INDEX) for Tomsk, Yeniseysk and Ufa governorates. According to family tradition, the brothers [Maksim and Pavel Trifonovich](/records/PUB-VGD-ANPILOGOV-BROTHERS-KOZHLI-STOLYPIN) from Oryol governorate, together with other settlers, founded the village of Kozhli near Tomsk; in 1898 Pavel is already recorded as its peasant. [Agafya Anpilogova left](/records/PUB-GAAK-ALTAI-INDEX-ANPILOGOVA-AGAFYA) from near Samara for the Altai, and in [Beyskaya volost](/records/GAKK-F160-O1-D2270-ANPILOGOV-BEYSKAYA-1911-1917) near the Sayan Mountains one more Anpilogov is recorded in 1911–1917.

But not everyone was looking for new ploughland. Others went to the towns, to the mines and factories, into military service and into the church clergy — that is the subject of the next chapter.

**Bakhtemir.** The nominal list of state peasants of 1843 (GAAO — State Archive of Astrakhan Oblast, f. 454, op. 1, d. 339): Mikhail Anpilogov's family «изъ Енотаевскаго уезда причислены въ 1837 году» (enrolled from Yenotayevsk district in 1837) and «въ селеніи Бахтемирскомъ оседло» (settled in the village of Bakhtemir).
