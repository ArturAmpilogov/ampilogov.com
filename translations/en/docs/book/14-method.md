# Continuing Your Own Family's Story

Starting from one rare name, this story has led to families, households and new towns. It can be continued from any of these places — from a person you know and the earliest record about them. The key is to understand what connection a document gives and what it does not yet contain.

## From a Line to a Conclusion

Each record card brings together the source, the date, a literal transcription, a modern reading and any available images. Pride of place goes to the scan that has been read. An index — a listing of names and dates that volunteers or text-recognition software compile on genealogy sites such as FamilySearch — helps to find the line; where it disagrees, its value is kept separately.

Thus, in a Simferopol record of 1868 the index gave Matrona an age of **0 days**, while the original says **8 days**. In another parish register entry, the daughter Lyubov was born on 30 December **1906**, although the baptism and the book itself belong to **1907**. The heading and neighbouring lines help to see where one year ends and the next begins. [Matrona](/records/FS-3Q9M-CS9N-9FBF) · [Lyubov](/records/FS-3Q9M-CSSV-K9K7-G).

## Three Degrees of Certainty

- **Established:** the source states the fact directly, and it is known whether it was read from the original, from a scholarly edition or from another kind of evidence.
- **Probable:** several independent indicators coincide, but the linking record has not yet been found.
- **Connection not established:** the surname, place or time are similar, but the document does not show a shared person or kinship.

The type of source matters too. The title of an archival file confirms its subject and dates, not all of its presumed contents. Information from a researcher's extract is checked against the original once it becomes available.

## Kinship Needs Its Own Words

The most reliable anchors are direct formulas: «сын» (son), «дочь» (daughter), «брат» (brother), «внук» (grandson). Akim's skazka (sworn statement) calls Tit his grandson but does not give the name of Tit's parent. So the chart keeps an unnamed generation. Inserting someone there just because the patronymic fits is not allowed. [Akim's story](08-akim-bazdyrevo-shchigry-1700.md).

Repeated records link a person through several features: first name, patronymic, the names of spouses, place, age and service. Surname variants are kept literally. The same form does not guarantee a common ancestor; different forms do not necessarily mean different families.

## Presence, Registration, Relocation

A parish register entry may place a native of one district (uyezd) in another town. It proves presence, but not necessarily permanent settlement. **Prichislenie (enrolment)** means administrative admission into a community. A petition to resettle does not yet confirm arrival.

That is why a line on the map appears only when there is a clear basis for it. For Kursk and Oboyan, Sergey's identity is probable; for Simferopol, the Rylsk district of Matrona's father is named directly. These connections differ in strength and meaning. ## Old Dates

Dates in the book are given as the source recorded them. Until 1918 Russia used the old Julian calendar: in the nineteenth century it was 12 days behind the present one, in the twentieth century 13 days. And before Peter I years were counted from the Creation of the World and began on 1 September, so such dates are written with a slash, for example 1594/95.

## If Your Family Is Not on the Site

The site holds only part of the records found so far, and many branches are still waiting for their document. It is best to search further step by step — from the known to the unknown.

**1. Start with the family.** Write down everything the older generation remembers: full names, dates, the villages and districts they came from. Go through the papers kept at home — birth and marriage certificates, military service cards, work-record books, inscriptions on the backs of photographs. The name of the village and the uyezd matters most: archives keep documents by place, not by surname.

**2. The twentieth century: ЗАГС and military databases.** Civil registration records are kept for a hundred years at the ЗАГС (civil registry office) and are then transferred to the state archive; relatives can request copies. The open databases [«Память народа» (Memory of the People)](https://pamyat-naroda.ru), [ОБД «Мемориал» (Memorial)](https://obd-memorial.ru) and [«Подвиг народа» (Feat of the People)](https://podvignaroda.ru) cover participants in the Great Patriotic War (the Soviet–German war of 1941–1945), and [«Память героев Великой войны» (Memory of the Heroes of the Great War)](https://gwar.mil.ru) covers the First World War. The fates of victims of repression can be traced in the [«Открытый список» (Open List)](https://openlist.wiki), and those deported to forced labour in Germany in the [Arolsen Archives](https://collections.arolsen-archives.org).

**3. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: parish registers and revisions.** [FamilySearch](https://www.familysearch.org/ru/) is a free database with millions of scans of parish registers (metricheskie knigi) and revision tales (census lists); only registration is required. A name search finds only indexed records; the other books are browsed through the catalogue: a place, for example Kursk Governorate, then the parish and the year. Some scans can only be opened at FamilySearch centres.

[Yandex Archive](https://yandex.ru/archive) searches directly through the text of handwritten parish registers, revision tales and confession lists (ispovednye vedomosti) from the archives of Moscow and of the Astrakhan, Orenburg, Irkutsk and other regions — including the places to which settlers from this book moved. Text recognition makes mistakes, so try every variant of the surname — Ampilogov, Anpilogov, Anfilogov, Onpilogov, Ampilov — and the old spelling with a final «ъ», and always check what you find against the scan.

Regional archives publish inventories (opisi) on their websites, and sometimes scans as well: [Kursk](https://archive.rkursk.ru), [Oryol](https://gaorel.ru), [Orenburg](https://orenarhiv.ru) and others. The project [«Наши предки» (Our Ancestors)](https://nashipredki.com) collects indexes of archival files by village, and on the [ВГД forum](https://forum.vgd.ru), which has sections for every region, people can help with reading old handwriting and suggest where to look.

**4. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.** Service lists, cadastral survey books (pistsovye knigi) and census books (perepisnye knigi) are held mainly in the [Russian State Archive of Ancient Acts](https://rgada.info) in Moscow, and much of them has been published in scholarly editions. Nineteenth-century files on resettlement, nobility and state peasants are in the [Russian State Historical Archive](https://fgurgia.ru) in St Petersburg.

**5. Write to the archive.** If the books you need have not been digitised, the archive can search for documents on request. In your letter, give the surname with all its variants, the names, approximate years, religion and the exact place — governorate, uyezd, volost, village or parish — and state the task: find a birth record, check a revision tale or send copies of a specific file. Genealogical requests are usually paid and take from a few weeks to several months. Archive addresses can be found on the website of [Rosarkhiv (the Federal Archival Agency)](https://archives.gov.ru). You can also do the work yourself: reading rooms admit visitors on presentation of an identity document — you just need to book in advance.

**6. Private researchers.** If you have no time to travel to archives, you can commission the search from a private genealogist or an archival agency. Choose those who send scans of documents with the full archival reference — fond, opis, delo, list (collection, inventory, file, folio) — and agree the scope of work and the price in advance. A beautifully designed tree without references to documents cannot be checked, and promises to "find the family's noble coat of arms" are best avoided.

The story of the whole surname grows each time another specific family is reconstructed. If you have found your branch or a document about bearers of the surname, [write to the author](/contact).
